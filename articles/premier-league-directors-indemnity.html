Who Pays If the Premier League Is Sued?

Alexei Ortelli · 19 November 2026 · 6 min read

Premier League clubs have voted to cover their directors' legal bills if Manchester City's sponsors sue them personally. The fix is sensible, if overdue, but the threats that prompted it look a good deal weaker than the headlines suggest.

On 19 November 2026, 18 of the Premier League's 20 clubs voted to amend the league's articles of association so that the clubs, rather than the league's five directors personally, will cover the directors' legal costs if any of Manchester City's sponsors take action against them, with only City and Nottingham Forest reportedly voting against [The Independent]. At 90 per cent, that margin comfortably cleared the two-thirds majority the vote needed [City AM], and the 75 per cent that the Companies Act 2006 sets for a special resolution [legislation.gov.uk]. The five directors covered, chief executive Richard Masters and chair Alison Brittain among them, sit atop a league that in September published its independent Commission's finding that City's financial rule breaches across nine seasons had a combined effect on revenue and costs of more than £900 million [Commission Core Decision].

I have covered the verdict itself here, so this article focuses on a narrower question. Two of City's most prominent commercial partners have reacted angrily to how they were described in that judgment: Etihad, after the commission labelled its contracts with City 'shams', has warned it may sue, while Etisalat has called the findings relating to it 'entirely false and defamatory' [City AM]. Given that neither has yet issued proceedings, the real questions are why the league's directors needed protecting at all, and whether they had much to fear in the first place.

What the law allows

The Premier League is, legally speaking, a private company wholly owned by its 20 member clubs [Premier League], and its directors are therefore subject to the same Companies Act rules as the directors of any other company. Section 232 of the Act starts from a strict position: any provision exempting a director from liability for negligence, default, breach of duty or breach of trust in relation to the company is void, and so is any attempt by the company to indemnify them against that liability [legislation.gov.uk]. However, section 234 carves out an important exception for a 'qualifying third party indemnity provision', which allows a company to cover its directors against liability to people outside the company [legislation.gov.uk].

That exception has clear limits. It cannot cover criminal fines or regulatory penalties, the costs of defending criminal proceedings that end in conviction, or the costs of losing a civil claim brought by the company itself [legislation.gov.uk]. A civil claim brought by a sponsor, a third party in every sense, sits squarely within what the law permits. Significantly, the league's rules had not previously covered its directors' legal costs if they were sued personally, a protection most private companies already provide [City AM]. Hence, in company law terms, the vote is less a dramatic intervention than a piece of overdue housekeeping, closing a gap that only became urgent once someone threatened to test it.

Would a claim succeed?

On what we know so far, the threatened claims face several serious hurdles. Etisalat's choice of the word 'defamatory' points towards a libel claim, and the first hurdle is set by section 1 of the Defamation Act 2013. A statement is only defamatory if it has caused or is likely to cause serious harm to the claimant's reputation, and for a body that trades for profit, that harm is not 'serious' unless it has caused or is likely to cause the body serious financial loss [legislation.gov.uk]. Thus Etihad or Etisalat would need to show more than embarrassment or bruised pride; they would need to prove real, quantifiable commercial damage flowing from the league's publication.

Moreover, the league would have strong defences available. The Defamation Act 1996 gives qualified privilege to a fair and accurate report of a finding or decision of an association formed to promote or safeguard the interests of a sport, where that association has power to control or adjudicate on those taking part in it [legislation.gov.uk]. A league publishing the conclusions of its own disciplinary commission looks like a natural fit for that protection. Additionally, the 2013 Act provides a defence where the imputation conveyed is substantially true [legislation.gov.uk], and another where a statement concerned a matter of public interest and the publisher reasonably believed publishing it was in the public interest [legislation.gov.uk]. Given that the findings came from an independent commission rather than from the directors themselves, and concern the financial integrity of English football's top division, both defences appear well suited to the league's position.

However, none of these defences is unconditional, and this is where the small print matters. The 1996 Act privilege is lost if the claimant shows the publication was made with malice, or if the league refuses a request to publish a reasonable statement by way of explanation or contradiction [legislation.gov.uk]. A shrewd sponsor might therefore begin not with a claim form but with a demand that the league publish its rebuttal. Crucially, the truth defence also rests on findings that City are still challenging, with an appeal expected to be heard next month [City AM]. If the appeal overturns the 'sham' findings, the league's position would weaken considerably. There is also a clock running: libel claims must be brought within one year of publication [legislation.gov.uk], so with the Commission's decision published on 29 September 2026, the sponsors cannot keep the threat hanging over the directors indefinitely.

Finally, it is worth asking why individual directors would be in the firing line at all. The natural defendant in a claim about a published judgment is the league that published it, not the people who sit on its board. The directors reportedly felt real anxiety about the prospect of personal claims [LiveScore, reporting The Times], and that may be precisely the point. Overall, a threat aimed at individuals, rather than at the institution, reads less like a carefully planned piece of litigation and more like an attempt to apply pressure where it is most likely to be felt.

Is the indemnity needed?

If the claims look weak, the vote might seem unnecessary. However, even a claim that is likely to fail can be expensive to defend, and a director facing personal exposure to legal costs has every reason to think twice before signing off on the next difficult decision. Masters has publicly described City as having systematically broken the league's rules for nearly a decade [The Independent], and no sanction has yet been decided [City AM]. Hence the indemnity's real value may lie less in the claims it guards against than in the decisions it protects, removing personal cost as a lever just as the league moves towards sanctioning its most powerful member.

Conclusion

Ultimately, the vote tells two stories at once. In company law terms, it is an orthodox and overdue use of section 234, giving the league's directors the kind of cover most boards already enjoy. In litigation terms, it guards against claims that, on what we know so far, would face a demanding financial loss threshold, statutory privilege and well-established defences, though a successful appeal by City could still change that picture. Either way, the episode is a useful reminder to any sports governing body that sets out to regulate powerful members: check your own constitution first, because the small print matters most when someone finally decides to test it.
