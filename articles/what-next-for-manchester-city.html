What Next for Manchester City?

Alexei Ortelli · 30 September 2026 · 9 min read

An independent Commission has found Manchester City guilty of serious breaches of the Premier League's financial rules across nine seasons in what looks to be the most significant controversy in English football history.

An independent Commission has found that Manchester City's income was overstated by more than £830 million across the nine seasons from 2009/10 to 2017/18, with the Premier League putting the combined effect on revenue and costs at more than £900 million [Commission Core Decision]. The hearing behind that finding lasted 42 days and produced approximately 7,000 pages of witness transcripts, yet the decision reached the public only on 29 September 2026, almost 22 months after the evidence closed in December 2024 [Premier League].

The Commission's findings

The Commission was asked to decide well over 100 alleged breaches, grouped by the Premier League into four charges. Charge 1 concerned the accounts City submitted to the League, Charge 2 UEFA's Club Licensing and Financial Fair Play Regulations, Charge 3 the League's own Profitability and Sustainability Rules, which cap losses at £105 million over a rolling three-year period, and Charge 4 the club's duties of co-operation and utmost good faith during the investigation [Commission Core Decision]. Every charge was found proven bar Charge 4(B).

At the centre of Charges 1 to 3 sits what the Commission calls the Disguised Funding Scheme, which it found was devised in early 2010. Under it, sponsorship agreements recorded fees well above fair market value, but the sponsor was liable to pay only a fraction, with the balance paid by Abu Dhabi United Group Investment & Development Ltd (ADUG), the club's owner, while the whole recorded fee was booked as commercial revenue. Given that owner equity contributions do not count towards income for UEFA's break-even calculation, and that the club was anxious not to break Chelsea's record single-season loss of £140 million set in 2006, the disguise was worth a great deal, and the Commission found it allowed City to avoid that record in 2009/10. Three further ADUG-funded arrangements, worth £8.866 million, £7.4 million and £0.5 million, kept liabilities off the club's books, while a circular arrangement with Fordham, an entity that purchased the club's players' image rights, meant £24.5 million was recorded as operating income when it was an equity contribution and £49.414 million of expenses went unrecorded [Commission Core Decision].

It would nonetheless be wrong to read the decision as a wholesale condemnation of everything the club did. The Commission accepted that Project Longbow, a 2012 exercise to boost revenue and cut operating losses, had many legitimate strands, with the Fordham arrangement the exception, and the Premier League accepted that certain early sponsorship deals were arm's length and at fair market value [Commission Core Decision]. The finding is a narrower and more damning one: that a specific mechanism was built to make the club look self-sufficient when it was not.

The sham finding

The Premier League's statement deliberately uses the word sham repeatedly. In English law the classic formulation comes from Diplock LJ in Snook v London and West Riding Investments Ltd [1967] 2 QB 786: a sham consists of acts done or documents executed that are intended to give third parties or the court the appearance of creating legal rights and obligations different from those the parties truly intend, and crucially all the parties must share that common intention [swarb.co.uk].

Given that requirement, a sham finding is demanding, since it must reach the mind of each counterparty as well as the club, and the sponsors concerned were not parties to these proceedings. The Commission's reasoning, however, appears built to withstand exactly that difficulty. On each limb of Charge 1 it found the agreements to be shams and then held, in the alternative, that if it was wrong the economic substance still differed from the legal form, so that the sums in question were owner equity or club expenses in any event. On Charges 2 and 3 it went further, finding that even if the sponsorship charge failed the agreements would fall to be restated to fair market value, and that the club would still have missed both sets of spending limits by a very substantial amount. Charge 1(D), the failure to disclose the agreements as related party transactions, did not depend on the sham finding at all [Commission Core Decision]. Thus, an appeal confined to the label would leave several routes to the same destination standing.

Witnesses and intent

Perhaps the most consequential findings are the ones about people rather than paperwork. The Commission concluded that evidence from a number of important witnesses called by the club was false in key respects, and that some of them had given evidence they knew to be untrue. It found that the club knew, or was reckless as to whether, its accounts gave a true and fair view, and that by its conduct the club 'clearly intended to circumvent' the League's rules. It also said it had not compartmentalised the charges, so that intentional misconduct proved on one led it to look more carefully at the others [Commission Core Decision]. That last observation is one City may seek to test on appeal, although it is equally consistent with a tribunal being candid about how a pattern of conduct colours its view of the evidence.

City's response

City's statement says the club is disappointed and surprised, maintains that it is innocent and promises to pursue every appeal avenue on the basis that the Commission's opinion contains clear material errors of law, principle and fact and is 'unsafe'. It adds that the club respected due process for eight years on the footing that the League would behave as an independent, impartial regulator free from partisan influence. However, the Commission has already answered that second point, rejecting City's criticisms of the investigation and prosecution as unjustified and finding that nothing the club raised amounted to a defence to any charge. Its challenges to the League's very ability to bring the charges failed too, including its challenge to Charge 2, which enforces UEFA's rules through the League's own Rule B.14.6, although the reasoning behind those rulings is also in appendices yet to be published [Commission Core Decision].

City will also point to history. In July 2020 the Court of Arbitration for Sport, after a three-day hearing, set aside UEFA's two-year European ban, stating that City had not disguised equity funding as sponsorship and that most of the alleged breaches were either not established or time-barred, while still imposing a reduced fine of €10 million for failing to co-operate. A Commission that heard 27 factual witnesses over 42 days has now reached a very different conclusion on an overlapping period. The two are not directly comparable, given that CAS was applying UEFA's regulations to the years 2012 to 2016 whereas the Commission applied the League's own rules across nine seasons, but the contrast is one City is likely to press. Its separate arbitration victory over the League's associated party transaction rules in 2024, in which two of the League's decisions on City sponsorship deals were found procedurally unfair, concerned different questions and does not determine whether these findings are sound, but it explains the language about impartiality.

Sanctions in comparable cases

No earlier case is a true guide to what City faces, but recent sanctions show the range and, more usefully, the factors that move it. The English baseline is the profit and sustainability cases. Everton exceeded permitted losses by £19.5 million and received a record 10-point deduction, cut to six on appeal, with a further two points following in April 2024 for a later period. Nottingham Forest exceeded its £61 million threshold by £34.5 million and lost four points, which stood on appeal. Both clubs admitted their breaches. The measures differ, since those cases concern excess losses and City's concern misstated income, so no ratio is offered here, but the gulf in scale and in character is plain: overspends measured in tens of millions and conceded, against a deliberate scheme found to have inflated income by hundreds of millions and a club that contested everything.

Chelsea's recent cases show what a transfer ban looks like in practice, and what moves it. In March 2026 the Premier League fined Chelsea a record £10.75 million over £47.5 million of secret payments made under Roman Abramovich between 2011 and 2018, which the club's new owners had self-reported, and imposed a nine-month ban on signing academy players, but avoided a points deduction [The Telegraph]. A ban on signing first-team players was suspended for two years [ICIJ], and the League identified the club's proactive self-reporting, admissions and exceptional co-operation as mitigating circumstances, noting that the club would not have breached the profit and sustainability rules even had the payments been disclosed. City is the mirror image on every mitigating factor, having contested the charges and been found to have made concerted efforts to frustrate the investigation. The earlier Chelsea episode is also instructive on appeals: in 2019 FIFA banned the club from registering players for two windows and fined it 600,000 Swiss francs over the registration of minors, and CAS cut the ban to one window, already served, and halved the fine, having found the club guilty of only about a third of the violations FIFA had identified.

Italy supplies the two European reference points, and it should be flagged that both arose under a different disciplinary system. The closer analogue is Juventus in 2023, punished for false accounting over transfer capital gains, where the federation's appeal court imposed 15 points although its own prosecutor had asked for nine, and banned former chairman Andrea Agnelli from football activities for two years [Sports Illustrated]. The deduction was then overturned by Italy's highest sporting court and replaced in May by a 10-point penalty. That complicates the Telegraph's suggestion that English commissions rarely exceed what the prosecuting body proposes, although it is a different system. The outer limit is Calciopoli in 2006, when Juventus was relegated to Serie B and stripped of its 2004/05 and 2005/06 titles for influencing referee appointments [CBS Sports], with an initial points deduction of 30 reduced to nine on appeal, and with the sporting courts sanctioning within about two months of the scandal breaking in May 2006. Calciopoli concerned sporting integrity rather than accounts, so it is a marker for what a governing body can do when it concludes a competition itself was compromised, not a prediction.

Overall, the comparators pull in different directions. English financial sanctions have so far been modest points deductions and fines, softened by admission and co-operation, while Italian scandals produced relegation and lost titles. City's case sits between the two: larger in scale and intent than anything decided in England, yet a different kind of wrong from Calciopoli, and with none of the mitigation that reduced Chelsea's punishment.

What City could face

The Commission's powers begin with a reprimand and run through unlimited fines, suspensions, points deductions and expulsion to 'such other order as it thinks fit', and they include compensation to another club or person with no set limit, according to the Telegraph's reading of the 2022/23 rules that experts believe still govern the case [The Telegraph]. The Premier League confirms that the list of available sanctions is not exhaustive [Premier League]. Hence the range is wide enough to accommodate almost any of the outcomes above, and the absence of a direct precedent is the point, with Simon Leaf of Three Points Law, describing the case as truly unprecedented. He also observed that Everton's sanction applied immediately and was varied on appeal, so a sanction here could equally take effect before any appeal is resolved.

Additionally, the sanction hearing will be private, and the League has said it will publish the outcome only once its rules permit, while stating its intention that the whole process, appeals included, concludes as soon as possible [Premier League].

Conclusion

Overall, the statement establishes something significant and leaves something undecided. The Commission has found, after a 42-day hearing and with reasoning layered so that it can survive the loss of any single argument, that City's accounts misstated its finances over nine seasons and that the club sought to frustrate the investigation. What it has not yet done is decide what follows, and that will fall to a sanction hearing the public cannot see, followed by an appeal in which City has staked its position on errors of law, principle and fact. Ultimately, the appeal may narrow the findings or trim a sanction, as Everton's and Chelsea's did, but City faces the harder task of persuading a board to disturb findings about credibility and intention, and if the pace of the last eight years is any guide, this process may run on for some time yet, with the sanction, when it arrives, the least predictable part of it.
