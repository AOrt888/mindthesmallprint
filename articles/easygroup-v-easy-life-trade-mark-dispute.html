Easy Life, Hard Lessons: What the easyGroup Trade Mark Dispute Teaches About Brand Protection

Alexei Ortelli · June 2025 · 8 min read

A Leicester indie band spent six years building a following under a name nobody else seemed to want, until the owner of easyJet decided otherwise. A year on from their enforced rebrand to Hard Life, the case is still one of the clearest lessons around in what a registered trade mark can, and cannot, do.

easyGroup, the licensing vehicle for Sir Stelios Haji-Ioannou's stable of "easy"-branded businesses, holds more than 250 registered trade marks in the UK alone, and many more worldwide, covering everything from easyJet and easyHotel to easyPizza and easyStorage [Harper James]. In 2023 alone it filed 14 trade mark lawsuits in the English courts, up from just two the year before, backed by a reported £4 million annual legal budget [City AM]. Sir Stelios has been open about the thinking behind the numbers, framing unauthorised use of "easy" as itself a back-handed compliment to the brand's strength, and reaching for a label that has stuck to the whole campaign: 'brand thieves' [City AM].

One of the 14 was a five-piece indie band from Leicester who had spent years building a career under a name they chose long before anyone at easyGroup noticed them: Easy Life.

A poster, a trade mark, and six years under one name

Easy Life formed in Leicester in 2017, signed to Island Records the following year, and by 2021 had two UK Top 10 albums to their name, Junk Food reaching number seven in 2020 and Life's a Beach reaching number two the following year. The tour promoting that second album, running across 2021 and into 2022, was named after it and was promoted with artwork styled deliberately on easyJet's own branding: an orange-and-white aeroplane livery, tour dates laid out like a departures board, and the tongue-in-cheek strapline "easy Life" set in the airline's own typeface. It is a clever piece of design, and it is also, as it turned out, the piece of artwork easyGroup would later put at the centre of its claim.

What the band could not easily have known when that tour was booked is that easyGroup had, on 14 September 2020, quietly filed a UK trade mark application for the word EASYLIFE (registration UK00003532904), covering an unusually broad 434 goods and services, from clothing and CDs to tableware and hunting equipment. The mark was registered on 19 August 2022, more than five years after Easy Life the band had already built a fanbase, a record deal and two albums under that name.

The claim

On 6 September 2023, easyGroup issued a High Court claim against the band's frontman Murray Matravers personally and against Easy Life as a firm, relying on four registered marks: the original easyJet word mark (1996), the easyJet aircraft livery mark (2002), an easyMusic mark covering entertainment services (2004) and the newly minted EASYLIFE mark itself (2022). The claim ran on three separate limbs of the Trade Marks Act 1994, and it is worth setting them out in full because each does a genuinely different job.

Section 10(1) catches use of an identical sign for identical goods or services; section 10(2) is broader, catching a sign that is identical or similar to the mark, used on identical or similar goods or services, "where because" of that similarity "there exists a likelihood of confusion on the part of the public". Both provisions are, at heart, about protecting consumers from being misled. Section 10(3) is a different animal entirely: it protects a mark with a reputation in the UK against use of a similar sign, even on completely dissimilar goods or services, where that use "takes unfair advantage of, or is detrimental to, the distinctive character or the repute of the trade mark". No confusion needs to be shown at all under section 10(3), only unfair advantage or detriment, which is precisely why it has become the provision of choice for a claimant like easyGroup, whose "easy" family of marks has a reputation that plainly extends well beyond the specific goods a defendant is selling.

easyGroup's pleaded case leaned into that distinction. On the EASYLIFE word mark itself, it argued straightforward confusion under sections 10(1) and 10(2): the band's merchandise, sold under the sign "Easy Life" on clothing, CDs and tableware, sat squarely within the goods the EASYLIFE registration actually covered. On the tour poster and the wider "easy" livery, it went further, arguing under section 10(3) that the orange-and-white aircraft imagery took unfair advantage of the reputation built up over three decades by easyJet, regardless of whether any fan was actually confused into thinking the airline had sponsored the tour.

Why Easy Life did not fight

Whatever the underlying merits, the band never tested them in court. By 10 October 2023 they had announced publicly that they could not afford to contest a High Court claim, and by the middle of that month they had played their final shows under the Easy Life name. Crucially, the risk was not confined to the band's finances as a business: because Matravers had been sued personally alongside the firm, an adverse costs order could, in principle, have reached his own assets rather than stopping at the band's.

What the case law says about easyGroup's odds

Had Easy Life fought on, the wider case law gives a genuine basis for thinking they might not have lost. In W3 Ltd v easyGroup Ltd [2018] EWHC 7 (Ch), a dispute with the operator of EasyRoommate, the High Court found that the word "EASY" on its own was descriptive and had not acquired distinctiveness, and went on to invalidate easyGroup's standalone EASY registration for the relevant services; distinctiveness, the court held, attached to "easy" only when combined with the specific white-on-orange livery, not to the word in isolation. The judgment also rejected easyGroup's "family of marks" argument outright, finding no likelihood of confusion between EasyRoommate and easyGroup's other marks because the added word was, in the court's words, conceptually, visually and aurally different from each of them.

Four years later, easyGroup fared little better in easyGroup Ltd v Nuclei Ltd [2022] EWHC 901 (Ch), a fight over the office broker Easy Offices, which had traded under that name since 2000. The High Court dismissed easyGroup's infringement claims and, on Nuclei's counterclaim, revoked easyGroup's own EASYOFFICE registrations for non-use, a finding the Court of Appeal largely upheld in easyGroup Ltd v Nuclei Ltd [2023] EWCA Civ 1247. Lord Justice Arnold's judgment on appeal is again notable for what it declined to do: even accepting that the two businesses offered identical services, he held that "Easy Offices" and "EASYOFFICE" differed enough in sound, appearance and concept that the difference would not go unnoticed by consumers, five years of side-by-side trading having produced no evidence of actual confusion at all.

Taken together, these two decisions suggest that Easy Life's strongest defence would have been on precisely the ground easyGroup's own newly filed EASYLIFE mark was weakest: distinctiveness. A mark registered only in 2022, for a name a working band had already used for five years without apparent objection, sits uneasily next to a High Court finding that "easy" plus an ordinary English word is not, without more, distinctive at all. The section 10(3) argument over the tour poster looks rather stronger in comparison, since it does not need to grapple with distinctiveness in the same way, and easyJet's own livery undeniably has the kind of reputation section 10(3) exists to protect. Significantly, though, trade mark law recognises no parody defence in the way copyright does for limited categories of use; poking fun at a famous brand's livery is not, on its own, a shield against an unfair-advantage claim, however knowing the joke.

Overall, easyGroup's record against parties who did fight, EasyRoommate and Easy Offices both won outright, suggests a claim against Easy Life was considerably less certain to succeed than the band's swift capitulation might imply. Hence the real lesson here may have less to do with the law's substance than with its cost: a claim need not be strong to work, if the other side cannot afford to find out how strong it actually is.

One year of Hard Life

On 11 June 2024, the band announced their new name, Hard Life, alongside a single called "Tears" written directly about the dispute, with Matravers explaining that the song was meant to capture the absurdity of the whole situation. easyGroup was reportedly unimpressed, describing the song as disparaging and a breach of the settlement reached between the parties, and expressing disappointment that the band had chosen to reopen a dispute it considered closed [Complete Music Update].

Ultimately, nothing in the easyGroup v Easy Life dispute was ever tested by a judge, which is itself the point. The Trade Marks Act 1994 gives a registered proprietor real and valuable rights, but as EasyRoommate and Easy Offices both show, those rights are far from unlimited once a defendant has the resources to hold their ground. For a band without those resources, the question was never really whether the claim would win. It was whether they could afford to find out.
