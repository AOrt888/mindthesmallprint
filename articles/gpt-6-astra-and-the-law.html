GPT-6 Astra and the Law

Alexei Ortelli · 15 September 2026 · 6 min read

OpenAI's newest flagship model launched with law firms as a headline use case. Here is what that actually means for the profession.

On 3 September 2026, OpenAI released GPT-6 Astra, its most capable model to date, pitched explicitly at "next generation work" [OpenAI]. Legal work was one of the showcased use cases, sitting alongside financial analysis, software engineering and design. Every major AI lab has now made the same choice within months of each other, so this is not a coincidence, and it says something about where the profession currently sits in frontier AI's commercial strategy.

A model built with lawyers in mind

The clearest evidence is Legora, an AI platform used by more than 100,000 professionals across some 1,800 in-house legal teams and law firms. Legora integrated Astra to automate financial statement tie-out, the process of checking every figure in a set of accounts against trial balances, consolidation schedules and prior-year records. In a demonstration run, the model worked through 41 documents in a single session and correctly flagged all four planted errors, including a £500,000 gap hidden in a revenue note [OpenAI]. Legora reported close to a 40% improvement over its previous model on that workflow specifically, a considerable jump for a single model generation.

Why the AI giants are suddenly chasing law firms

Legora is one story among many. Since February 2026, Anthropic has launched Claude for Legal and expanded it with more than 20 connectors and a dozen practice area plugins. Google released Gemini Enterprise for Legal in August 2026, naming Freshfields, the London-headquartered Magic Circle firm, among its launch customers, and Freshfields has since reported more than 5,000 of its own professionals using Gemini-built tools. In London, Magic Circle partner rates have reportedly climbed to between £1,000 and £1,500 an hour [Law Gazette], making lawyers an unusually high value seat to sell software into. General counsel adoption of generative AI has jumped from 20% in 2023 to 87% today, a shift in under three years that most areas of legal practice would not see in a decade [FTI Consulting].

From assistant to agent

What makes Astra different from earlier legal AI tools is that it can be set loose on a multi-step task, work through documents, use software and hand back a finished piece of analysis with comparatively little supervision along the way. OpenAI reports a 20% improvement in end-to-end task completion and states that Astra needs less scaffolding to work reliably as a proactive agent [OpenAI].

Agentic tools sit uneasily against the Solicitors Regulation Authority's own conduct rules, which assume a human solicitor exercises independent judgement on every matter. The SRA underlined exactly this in a warning notice issued on 17 August 2026, reminding firms that "you remain accountable for all work and advice delivered to clients, whether or not AI has been used", and flagging client confidentiality as a related risk wherever information is entered into a public AI tool and stored, retained or used to improve it [SRA].

What does this mean for intellectual property and the future of the legal profession?

English copyright law already has an answer for who owns an AI-assisted work, and it predates all of this by decades. Section 9(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 assigns authorship of a "computer-generated work" to "the person by whom the arrangements necessary for the creation of the work are undertaken", a provision most other jurisdictions do not have. It has never been tested in court against a system that makes several of its own decisions along the way, which means any advice a firm gives a client on who owns an agent-assisted work product is, for now, a considered opinion. Pinsent Masons has made much the same point about tort more broadly, arguing that the deceit-based liability rules courts would otherwise reach for were not built for a system capable of acting autonomously, per the UK Jurisdiction Taskforce's own admission that courts "will find it more difficult to impose liability on AI supply chain parties without development of existing tort law norms" [Pinsent Masons].

Patent law has at least given a clear answer, and it is not a reassuring one. In Thaler v Comptroller-General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks [2023] UKSC 49, the Supreme Court held that section 7 of the Patents Act 1977 requires an inventor to be a natural person, so Dr Thaler's AI system, DABUS, could not be named as the inventor of its own creations, however autonomous the invention process had genuinely been. As tools like Astra move from answering questions to running independent research workflows, that gap becomes real: an invention a model meaningfully originates may end up with no route to patent protection at all.

The clearest sign that the risk is real came from the English High Court. In Getty Images v Stability AI [2025] EWHC 2863 (Ch), the court found no secondary copyright infringement, but it did find Stability AI liable for trade mark infringement, after AI-generated images reproduced a distorted version of Getty's own watermark. That a developer with every incentive to avoid exactly this outcome ended up on the wrong side of trade mark law without ever intending to is the clearest evidence yet that this risk sits inside ordinary use.

The liability question layers on top of the ownership question, and it will decide who actually carries this risk. Ashfords has argued that the real legal issue with agentic AI is no longer just what a model says, but what it is authorised to do, who supervises it and what evidence exists that a business stayed in control, a standard that maps directly onto infringement, not just onto internal governance [Ashfords]. The Competition and Markets Authority's own March 2026 guidance settles what happens once that standard is not met: "a business is responsible for what an AI agent does in the same way as it would be for what an employee does, including where a third party has designed or provides the AI agent" [CMA]. Read against Getty, that turns the watermark finding from a cautionary tale about a developer into a live question of exposure for any firm running an agent across IP-adjacent work of its own.

None of this involves Astra directly, and almost none of it is resolved. That should be read as a warning. There is undoubtedly more work here for IP lawyers, advising on authorship, ownership, infringement and liability risk that barely existed five years ago. But that growing docket exists because English law has fallen behind the technology it is meant to govern and the same gap will shape the wider profession long after this particular model has been superseded. The firms that build real authority, oversight and an audit trail into how they use tools like Astra will be judged very differently, by regulators and clients alike, from those that simply chased the productivity gains.
