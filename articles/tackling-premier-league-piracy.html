Tackling Premier League Piracy

Alexei Ortelli · August 2024 · 8 min read

With illegal streaming more widespread and sophisticated than ever, the Premier League faces a very real and developing threat to its broadcasting model. This piece is a condensed version of my MA Law dissertation, titled Tackling Premier League Piracy: Assessing the Effectiveness of UK Copyright Law and Remedies in Combatting Illegal Premier League Streaming.

The “Industry Killer”

The English Premier League is now the most-watched football league on earth, with a global audience now north of 1.85 billion fans [Premier League].

That reach translates directly into revenue: broadcasting deals now make up 53% of club income [Deloitte], making the Premier League Europe's highest-earning domestic competition.

But the value of those deals rests entirely on one thing: exclusivity, and that exclusivity is being chipped away by two very different pressures driving fans toward piracy.

The first is structural. English football's long-standing 3pm Saturday blackout, a rule designed to protect matchday attendances, keeps over 100 Premier League fixtures off UK screens entirely every season. The second is economic: the cost-of-living squeeze, paired with rising subscription prices, has pushed an estimated one in six UK adults [The Athletic] toward illegal streams simply to watch the football they cannot otherwise access or afford.

The result is the same either way: broadcasters pay for exclusive access, and piracy quietly erodes it. One 2019 study put the cost of piracy at £1 million per match [SportsPro]. It is a figure stark enough that some in the industry have started calling piracy exactly what it is: an industry killer [The Times].

Defending Against Piracy – The CDPA

The starting point is the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 (CDPA). Section 6 defines a broadcast as an electronic transmission of images or sounds for simultaneous public reception, and live Premier League coverage fits that comfortably. Section 6(1A) extends the same protection to simultaneous internet streams, which matters given how much football is now watched through an app rather than a set-top box. Section 20 then gives broadcasters the exclusive right to "communicate the work to the public," and it is this right that pirate streams infringe.

The courts have read that right broadly. In Football Association Premier League Ltd v British Sky Broadcasting Ltd [2013], the first English case to tackle illegal sports streaming, the court held that FirstRow Sports, a site that did not host any streams itself but aggregated links to them, was still responsible for communicating the broadcasts to the public because its operators actively intervened by inviting and aggregating those streaming links [FAPL v BSkyB]. In other words, curating piracy counts as piracy, even if you never touch the stream yourself.

That same logic caught two IPTV services in Columbia Pictures Industries Inc v British Telecommunications Plc [2024], even though neither hosted the content directly. Given that most people pirating football go through exactly this kind of aggregator rather than a raw stream, that breadth matters a great deal.

Post-Brexit, the CDPA's reach also holds up internationally. The Berne and Rome Conventions bind the UK regardless of EU membership and English courts have repeatedly found jurisdiction over foreign sites simply by asking whether the infringing content is targeted at the UK public, weighing factors like an English-language site, a large share of UK traffic or UK-targeted advertising [FAPL v BT].

On paper, then, UK copyright law is doing its job. The real problem is not the law itself; it is enforcing it.

Launching a Counterattack

The CDPA's main tool against piracy is the blocking injunction under section 97A, which forces Internet Service Providers (ISPs) to cut off access to infringing sites. But not all blocking injunctions are equal.

Static injunctions were the first of their kind, used in the FirstRow case to block the site's IP address. The problem: IP addresses often host hundreds of unrelated, entirely legal websites, so taking one down can mean collateral damage on a large scale.

Live blocking injunctions, developed in FAPL v British Telecommunications Plc [2017], fixed that. Rather than a permanent ban, ISPs block and unblock streaming servers in real time, only for the duration of the match itself. This has been genuinely effective: more than 600,000 illegal streams were blocked during the 2022/23 season alone, three times the number blocked in 2019 [The Athletic].

Dynamic injunctions, the newest development from Sky UK Ltd v British Telecommunications Plc [2023], go a step further still. Once granted, rights holders can apply blocking measures whenever and wherever they choose, without going back to court each time. It is faster and more adaptive, but it also hands rights holders more discretion than courts have traditionally been comfortable with, and questions around proportionality remain largely untested.

Even the best of these tools has a ceiling, though. VPNs and other circumvention methods mean blocking injunctions can only ever shrink the problem, not solve it. And beyond the courtroom, alternative measures like the Digital Economy Act 2010's notice-and-penalty system, the Creative Content UK education campaign and intensified policing (including the Flawless IPTV prosecution, which saw its operators jailed for over 30 years [FACT UK]) all help at the margins, but none of them meaningfully move the number of people pirating football.

How to Move the Needle

If enforcement alone cannot fix the piracy problem, the fix has to address why people pirate in the first place. Two changes would go further than any injunction.

Firstly, scrap the 3pm blackout. Right now, over 100 matches a season are legally unwatchable in the UK no matter what you are willing to pay. The EFL's decision to abandon its own blackout for the 2024/25 season offers a live test case worth watching closely.

Secondly, build a unified, affordable streaming platform. OFCOM has found that cost is the single biggest reason people pirate, with 32% of infringers saying they would stop if a cheaper legal option existed [OFCOM]. It is exactly what happened in music: streaming services like Spotify are credited with cutting piracy by as much as 20% in some markets [Alliotts]. Football has yet to have its Spotify moment.

As Valve founder Gabe Newell has put it in a much-quoted line on games piracy, the way to beat piracy generally is not more enforcement technology; it is offering people a service that is better than what the pirates are providing.

UK copyright law is, in theory, a genuinely effective weapon against football piracy. In practice, the law is only ever going to be one half of the solution. The other half is making legal football cheaper and easier to watch than the alternative.
