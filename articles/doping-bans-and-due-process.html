Doping, Bans and Due Process in Elite Sport

Alexei Ortelli · 15 August 2026 · 10 min read

A positive drugs test makes headlines in a single line — name, substance, ban. What actually happens between the test and that headline is a detailed legal process, built around rules that most sports fans never see and that regularly divide opinion on whether they're fair at all.

Strict liability: guilt without intent

The starting point, under Article 2.1 of the World Anti-Doping Code, is strict liability. If a prohibited substance or its metabolites are found in an athlete's sample, that alone is an anti-doping rule violation — it doesn't matter whether the athlete took it deliberately, took it by accident or didn't know it was in their body at all. The Code's justification is consistency: intent is notoriously hard to prove, and a system that required it would be easy to game. The cost of that consistency is that an athlete can be sanctioned for something they didn't knowingly do.

The immediate fallout: provisional suspension

An adverse finding for most prohibited substances triggers a mandatory provisional suspension before any hearing takes place. The athlete is out of competition immediately, on a precautionary basis — not yet a finding of guilt, but functionally identical to one in its effect on their season, sponsorships and, in some cases, their career timing around events like the Olympics.

Flipping the burden of proof

Once presence is established, the burden shifts to the athlete. To avoid or reduce the standard sanction — typically a four-year ban for a deliberate violation — they must prove how the substance got into their system and establish either “no fault or negligence” or “no significant fault or negligence” under Article 10 of the Code. That's a marked departure from the presumption of innocence familiar from criminal law. Hearing panels decide these questions to the civil standard of “comfortable satisfaction” — a threshold sitting above the ordinary balance of probabilities but below the criminal standard of beyond reasonable doubt.

Getting a hearing

Athletes are entitled to a hearing before an independent, impartial panel before any final sanction is confirmed — in the UK, this is the National Anti-Doping Panel; international federations and major events run their own equivalent tribunals. Legal representation, disclosure of the evidence, and a reasoned decision are all part of the process, even if the strict liability starting point limits what a hearing can ultimately change.

The final word: the Court of Arbitration for Sport

Decisions can be appealed to the Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS) in Lausanne, sport's equivalent of a final appellate court. One of the most closely watched recent examples is figure skater Kamila Valieva's case: in January 2024, CAS's Anti-Doping Division found she had committed an anti-doping rule violation after testing positive for trimetazidine, imposed a four-year period of ineligibility backdated to the date of the test, and disqualified her results from the event — including the 2022 Beijing team competition. The case was widely discussed partly because Valieva was fifteen at the time of the test, engaging the Code's separate provisions for “protected persons”, and it illustrates how a single sample can take years to work through the full hearing and appeal process.

Where the fairness debate lives

Critics of the current system point to several recurring flashpoints: strict liability catching athletes who ingested a substance through contaminated supplements or meat, rather than deliberate doping; the practical advantage well-funded athletes have in mounting a scientific defence compared with those without access to expert toxicologists; and the “whereabouts” system, under which three missed tests or filing failures within twelve months can itself amount to a violation, with no positive test required at all. Supporters counter that a system flexible enough to weigh every athlete's individual story would be too slow and too easy to exploit to actually deter doping.

What both sides tend to agree on is the underlying tension: anti-doping law is built for speed and consistency across every sport and every country, and speed and consistency don't always sit comfortably with fairness to the individual case in front of the panel. That tension, rather than any single rule, is the real subject of most doping appeals.
